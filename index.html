About

What I do, and how I decide what not to build.

I'm a Product Manager based in London. Six years across AI/ML, B2B SaaS and e-commerce — three of them owning an ML recommendation product running in production for 1,000+ merchants, then eighteen months taking a B2B2C SaaS platform from problem definition to a live MVP as the only PM on it. Most recently I led growth and AI automation for a UK platform with 127k+ monthly active users.

What I actually do is find the constraint. At Rosetta.ai the interesting question was never whether the model was accurate — it was which client segment personalisation would pay off for, before we spent a year rolling it out to everyone. At JUKSY the growth ceiling wasn't traffic, it was how many articles an editor could physically write in a day.

I work hands-on with data, model evaluation and agentic tooling (Claude Code, Codex) to de-risk decisions before engineering time gets committed — and I ship my own products on the side to keep that honest. Most AI products fail in the translation to commercial outcomes, not in the modelling. That translation is the part I do.

  • 01Find the constraint before writing the spec

    Every roadmap I've owned started by naming the one thing actually capping the business — throughput, margin, activation — rather than working down the longest feature request list. It's what stops a team shipping the wrong thing beautifully.

  • 02De-risk before engineering time is committed

    I'll build the rough version myself: a data pull, a model evaluation, a working prototype. The team then commits to a direction that has already been tested, not to a hypothesis in a document.

  • 03Ship to a number, not to a date

    Every launch I've run had a metric attached before it shipped and an honest read on it after — conversion, AOV, output per editor-day, engagement time. If nothing moved, that is also a finding, and it changes the next decision.

08Data productScoping under constraint2026

Travel Radar — Flight price agent

A price agent watching London ⇄ Taipei fares for one specific trip, alerting only when a fare crosses a threshold worth acting on. The interesting work was product scoping, not scraping: the obvious data sources had just died.

The constraint

Halfway through, the two viable data sources disappeared: one major flight API closed its self-service tier and another moved to invite-only. What remained could answer simple return queries reliably and multi-city queries not at all.

The product decision

Rather than ship a multi-city feature that would silently return nothing, I cut it from v1 and redesigned the trip itself around split tickets — a separate short-haul return plus a long-haul return — which delivers the same "stop somewhere on the way" outcome with no missed-connection risk. A feature that fails quietly is worse than a feature you don't have.

How it runs

A scheduled job checks fares, compares them against thresholds set per routing type, and pushes to Telegram only on a breach, plus one weekly summary. Nothing else. The success metric was deliberately "messages I act on", not "messages sent".

09LLM productUnit economics2026

Daily Brief — Personalised morning briefing bot

A Telegram bot that writes each reader a different morning briefing — world news, the London job market, and a running finance-literacy series — and lets them change their own settings by simply telling it, in plain language.

The architecture decision that mattered

The naive design — run the whole pipeline once per reader — would have multiplied cost by roughly 50× at the target reader count. I split it into a shared research pass that builds a candidate pool once, then a cheap per-reader pass that selects from the pool and writes the personalised framing. Cost now barely moves as readers are added, which is what made the product viable at all.

Latency where it's felt

Sign-up used to wait for the nightly batch, so a new reader got silence. I moved the conversational onboarding to an edge worker running pure rules — instant and free — and left the language-model work in the batch. Settings changes are explicitly next-day, and the bot says so rather than pretending to be instant.

The data decision

The first version collected structured demographics. I replaced all of it with one free-text self-description: it gives the model far better context than a set of labels ("currently retraining as a PM" beats "occupation: marketing"), and it keeps the product out of the territory where UK GDPR would demand much more of me. Less data, better output.
