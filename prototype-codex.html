About

What I do, and how I decide what's worth building.

I'm a Product Manager based in London. Six years across AI, SaaS and e-commerce: three years owning an ML recommendation product used in production by 1,000+ merchants, then six months taking a B2B2C SaaS platform from problem definition through prototype and MVP to a live product, as its only PM. Most recently, I led growth and AI automation for a UK platform with 127k+ monthly active users.

What I do is find the constraint. At Rosetta.ai, the question was never whether the model was accurate. It was which client segment would actually benefit enough from personalisation to make it worth rolling out, before we spent a year taking it to everyone. At Banyan and JUKSY, the ceiling on growth wasn't traffic; it was how much content one editor could produce in a day.

I focus on the result, working hands-on with data, model evaluation and agentic tooling to test product directions before engineering commits to them. I also build and run my own products on the side to keep that honest. Most AI products fail not in the modelling, but in translating it into a commercial outcome. That translation is the part I do.

  • 01Clarify goals and constraints first

    Every roadmap I've owned started by naming the one thing actually capping the business, whether that was throughput, margin or activation, rather than working down the longest list of feature requests. Define the problem, break it into parts, then decide what gets built and in what order.

  • 02Ship the smallest version, then scale it

    I'd rather put a working prototype in front of real users than defend a hypothesis in a document. At JUKSY that meant a prototype, then an MVP live in the market, then feature work shaped by user feedback and test results rather than by what we assumed they wanted, with a metric agreed before launch and an honest read on it after.

  • 03Automate the repetitive work

    Any process that runs the same way twice stops being product work and becomes something to automate. Doing that at Banyan doubled output per editor at flat headcount and gave the team its time back for the parts that needed judgement.

Challenge

Clients relied on generic bestseller and frequently-bought-together widgets that ignored individual style preference. In fashion this fails harder than in most categories: catalogues turn over every season, and what a shopper buys depends on taste rather than spec, so popularity-based merchandising surfaces the wrong things to almost everyone. The result was poor discovery, low AOV and high product-page bounce.

My role

As Product Specialist and fashion domain expert I owned the lifecycle: defining model requirements with data science, building the data infrastructure, designing the A/B testing framework, and coordinating rollout across 1,000+ merchants. I built the 10,000+ attribute taxonomy and database from consumer research across the fashion industry, which lifted product recognition accuracy from 40% to 90%+ across all categories and gave the ranking model its benchmark. The engine reads browsing patterns, style affinity and purchase intent to surface per-user recommendations in real time, at 43% lower inference latency than the previous production solution.

Outcome

Merchants moved from static merchandising to per-user discovery. Early-adopter accounts migrating from zero personalisation saw revenue grow 660% and conversion rate 350% within their first year, with inference latency 43% below the previous production solution.

05LLM productUnit economics2026

Daily Brief — Personalised morning briefing bot

A Telegram bot that delivers the day's news at a set time, filtered by the industries you care about and capped at however many stories you want. It also handles learning tracks: pick a subject like accounting or finance and it builds a daily study plan alongside the briefing.

The constraint
The interesting part was the cost. Running an agent through several WebSearch calls and having it read each result end to end burns tens of thousands of tokens on search output alone.

Solution
Switching to a script that pulls RSS first, returning forty headlines and summaries as cheap plain text, then handing the model a single select-and-write pass, cut token use by five to ten times and made the output more consistent.
